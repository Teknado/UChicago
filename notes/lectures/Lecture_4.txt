F-test for Model Evaluation
Recall the Multiple Linear Regression model
Y = β0 + β1X1 . . . + βdXd + ε,
ε ∼N(0, σ2)
F-test concerns with a big picture question:
Is this regression worthwhile? Does it help us predict Y ?
H0 : β1 = β2 = . . . = βd = 0
H1 : at least one βj̸ = 0.
Assess if the overall model is statistically significant in explaining the data
4

===== PAGE 5 =====
F-test Statistic
Construct the F test statistic from regression ANOVA:
f = SSR/(p −1)
SSE/(n −p) =
R2/(p −1)
(1 −R2)/(n −p)
If f is big, then the regression is “working”:
▶Big SSR relative to SSE.
▶R2 close to one.
As usual, we need a statistical notion of “how big is big”. Under H0, f has Fp−1,n−p
distribution with p −1 numerator and n −p denominator degrees of freedom.
▶Generally, f > 4 is very significant (reject the null).
5

===== PAGE 6 =====
The F-Distribution
What kind of distribution is this?
It is a right skewed, positive valued family of distributions indexed by two parameters
(the two df values).
The p-value for this test is φ = Pr(Fp−1,n−p > f ).

Partial F-test
With the F-test example we were asking
“Is this regression worthwhile?”
Now, we’re asking
“Is it useful to add these extra covariates to the regression?”
Partial F-test compares two nested models and decide if the additional predictors or
complexity in the larger model are justified.
You always want to use the simplest model possible.
▶Only add covariates if they are truly informative or compulsory.

Partial F-test
Consider the regression model
Y = β0 + β1X1 . . . + βdbaseXdbase + βdbase+1Xdbase+1 . . . + βdfullXdfull + ε
Such that dbase is the number of covariates in the base (small) model and dfull > dbase
is the number in the full (larger) model.
The Partial F-test is concerned with the hypotheses
H0 : βdbase+1 = βdbase+2 = . . . = βdfull = 0
H1 : at least one βj̸ = 0 for j > dbase.

===== PAGE 16 =====
F-test vs t-test
Suppose that you have d covariates, X1 . . . Xd, and you are considering adding a new
covariate Xd+1 into your model.
The t-test is H0 : βd+1 = 0 vs H1 : βd+1̸ = 0, with test statistic z = bd+1/sbd+1 and
p-value P(Zn−d−2 > |z|).
Partial F-test is H0 : βd+1 = 0 vs H1 : βd+1̸ = 0, with test stat
f = (SSEfull −SSEbase)/MSEfull and p-value P(F1,n−d−2 > f ).
The test hypotheses are exactly the same!
16

It turns out that the tests also lead to the same p-values.
Consider testing the two covariates (ik and ltr) regression against an even more
simple model that includes only ik.
The t-test asks: is bltr far enough from zero to be significant?
F-test asks: is the increase in R2 significantly big?

===== PAGE 18 =====
F-test vs t-test
Ask the same question, F and t tests give the same answer!
In fact, f = z2 and P(Zn−d−2 > |z|) = P(F1,n−d−2 > z2),
so the f stat for one extra variable is just a squared t stat.

===== PAGE 19 =====
F-test vs t-test
Why not always decide what to include by just looking at individual t-stats?
Multicollinearity: If the X’s are highly correlated with each other, then sbj’s will be
very big (since you don’t know which Xj to regress onto), and you will fail to reject
βj = 0 for all of the Xj’s even if they do have a strong effect on Y .
Does F-test significance imply at least one t-test significance? How about vice versa?

Variable Selection
Suppose we observe Y , an outcome of interest, and (X1, ..., Xd), a set of potential
explanatory variables or predictors.
Assuming the model is always linear, so model selection simplifies to variable selection.
The variable selection problem:
To find the ”best” subset of X’s for predicting Y
Particularly of interest when d is large and X1, ...Xd is thought to contain many
redundant or irrelevant variables.

===== PAGE 23 =====
Guiding Principle for Variable Selection
Ockham’s Razor: Plurality ought not be posited without necessity.
Brevity is the soul of wit.
Overly complicated models lead to bad forecasts.

===== PAGE 24 =====
Variable Selection
We need to find a trade-off between data fit and simplicity.
We already know one method: partial F-test!
▶Do your regression with covariates you think should be in the model.
▶Kick out the variables that don’t seem significant.
▶Use a partial F-test to see if the simple model is good enough.
This only works for a small number of variables, which you’ve chosen intelligently.

===== PAGE 25 =====
Multiple Testing
A big problem with using tests (t or F) for comparing models is the false discovery
rate associated with multiple testing:
▶If you do 20 tests of true H0, with α = .05, you expect to see 1 positive (i.e. you
expect to reject at true null).
Suppose that you have 100 predictors, 10% of which are actually influential, and that
you find all of those significant.
Since you reject H0 for 5% of the useless 90% variables, 4.5/(4.5 + 10) ≈1/3 of your
significant bj’s will be false positives!
In modern big data problems, <1% of variables are influential.

===== PAGE 29 =====
Information Criteria for Model Selection
Instead of testing, there are Information Criteria metrics which attempt to quantify
how well our model would have predicted the data (regardless of what you’ve
estimated for the βj’s).
The best of these is the BIC: Bayes Information Criterion, which is based on a
“Bayesian” philosophy of statistics.
BIC = n log(s2) + p log(n),
where p = d + 1 = ‘degrees of freedom’ used in your model fit.
You want to choose the model that leads to minimum BIC.
29

===== PAGE 30 =====
AIC for Model Selection
Another popular metric is the Akaike Information Criterion:
AIC = n log(s2) + 2p
A general form for these criterion is n log(s2) + kp, where k = 2 for AIC and
k = log(n) for BIC.
In Python, we can get the AIC and BIC as follows.
> reg full = smf.ols(formula=’ret ∼
{}’.format(rest full), data = F1).fit()
> full AIC = reg full.aic
> full BIC = reg full.bic
full model AIC: -61.02
full model BIC: -26.46
AIC prefers more complicated models than BIC, and it is not as easily interpretable.

===== PAGE 40 =====
Forward Stepwise Regression
Forward stepwise procedures: start from a simple ‘null’ model, and incrementally
update fit to allow slightly more complexity.
The forward selected() function executes a common routine:
▶Fit all univariate models. Choose that with lowest AIC
and put that variable – say x(1) – in your model.
▶Fit all bivariate models including x(1) (y ∼β(1)x(1) + βjxj), and add xj from one
with lowest AIC to your model.
▶Repeat: min AIC by adding one variable to your model.
You stop when some model selection rule (AIC) is lower for the current model than for
any of the models that add one variable.
40

===== PAGE 41 =====
Better than backwards methods
▶The ‘full’ model can be expensive or tough to fit, while the null model is usually
available in closed form.
▶Jitter the data and the full model can change dramatically (because it overfits).
The null model is always the same.
Stepwise approaches are ‘myopic’: they find the best solution at each step without
considering the overall global path.
A related subtle (but massively important) issue is stability.
Stepwise procedure has high sampling variability: the selected variables change a lot
from one dataset to another.

Out-of-Sample Prediction
How do we evaluate a prediction model? Make predictions!
Basic Idea: We want to use the model to predict outcomes for observations we have
not seen before.
▶Use the data to create a prediction problem.
▶See how our candidate models perform.
We’ll split the entire sample into training, validation, and test subsamples.

===== PAGE 43 =====
Training, Validation, and Test
1. Training Phase (model building)
▶The model learns from the training set by adjusting its parameters (e.g., coefficients
in a regression model).
2. Validation Phase (model selection)
▶During training, different models or hyperparameters are evaluated using the
validation set to determine the best model configuration.
3. Testing Phase (model evaluation)
▶After model selection, the test set is used to evaluate the final model. This step
provides a realistic assessment of how the model will perform on unseen data.
▶The test set remains untouched until this stage to avoid biased estimates of model
performance.
1: In-sample; 3: Out-of-sample;
2: gray zone. “In-” for model evaluation purpose, “Out-of-” for model selection.
