## Lecture 3 — Diagnostics and Transformations, Categorical Variables, Interactions, Multiple Linear Regressions

Lecture 3 has two halves. Pages 2–18 cover diagnostics for simple regression. It restates the model assumptions (linear conditional mean; errors that are normal, independent and of constant variance) and warns that "inference and prediction relies on this model being true" (p.3). Anscombe's quartet (p.4–8) shows why you plot the data and the residual-vs-fitted plot ("your #1 tool for finding fit problems", p.8). It then covers leverage, standardized and studentized residuals, the |r| > 2.5 outlier flag, the with-and-without-outliers policy, and the warning that high-leverage outliers hide from r_i. Pages 19–58 cover multiple regression: the model and how to read a partial effect, least squares and its closed form, `smf.ols`, the Welch–Goyal equity-premium regression, coefficient inference (S_b, t, CIs), R², prediction intervals, and multicollinearity (the exact case on p.40; infl/lty/tbl "individually insignificant, jointly significant" on p.41–44). It ends with the orange-juice example: dummy variables and reference levels, log and log-log transforms, interactions (`*`), confounding, and the ŷ-vs-y fit plot. Every slide works in sample; nothing in L3 covers out-of-sample evaluation.

- **C1 — L3 p.40 (cell 56 print; the same reasoning drives cell 65's "its rebuild x86 − x12 is spanned" and cell 96 §2 item 3's "it is an exact combination of two included series"). - **C2 — L3 p.12–18 (cell 63, characteristics row). MINOR, stretched.** The claim: "Ranks are bounded, so no single extreme value can dominate a least-squares fit (L3 p.12–18 shows how far one point can move one)."
