## Write-up

# Characteristics, macro and cross-country asset returns: a pre-registered out-of-sample study

*Every number below is printed by a cell above; Table 3.31 collects the headline numbers. Tables and figures are numbered as in Sections 3.0–3.10. Lecture citations, here and throughout the notebook, are PDF page numbers; in Lectures 1, 5 and 8 the number printed on a slide can be lower, because of overlays.*

**Abstract.** We ask three questions of a panel of 50 assets in four classes and 12 countries, 2000–2024. Can next month's excess return be forecast from five lagged characteristics? Does macro information add anything? Does a forecast-based portfolio beat simple rules after costs? The design, the tests and the decision rules were fixed before any model was fitted, and 38 candidate specifications are scored on 168 out-of-sample months (2011–2024). No specification shows detectable predictability. The pre-registered ridge model has an out-of-sample R² of 0.04% (SE 0.24%) against the trailing mean, and the characteristics add nothing to a model of class means alone. Macro lowers R² in every paired comparison and does no better than the same series shifted in time; interactions of the characteristics with macro states, checked after the audit, add nothing either. Even with the penalty chosen in hindsight, no linear model beats the class means. The forecast portfolio earns a higher net Sharpe ratio than equal weight, risk parity and time-series momentum (0.44 against 0.12–0.30), but its α is not significant (t = 1.74). Its weights come from the class means, not the characteristics, and the gain is associated with re-estimating those means each year.

## 1. Introduction

A desk that trades across countries and asset classes has to decide whether a forecasting model is worth paying for, or whether cheap rules that use only past returns already capture what is there. This study asks three questions:
- **Q1.** How much of next month's excess return is forecastable from lagged asset characteristics?
- **Q2.** Does global or country macro add anything?
- **Q3.** Does a forecast-based portfolio beat equal weight, risk parity and time-series momentum out of sample, after costs?

The literature says there should be something to find, but not much. Value and momentum premia appear across many markets and asset classes (Asness, Moskowitz and Pedersen, 2013), and an asset's own past 12-month return predicts its next return across futures markets (Moskowitz, Ooi and Pedersen, 2012); the time-series momentum rule we test against is built on that finding. Gu, Kelly and Xiu (2020) forecast US stock returns with machine-learning models that combine firm characteristics with macro predictors, and reach monthly out-of-sample R² of a few tenths of a percent. The course's sanity range for monthly stock-level R²_OOS is 0.3–0.5% (L5 p.57), and Lecture 8 warns that "when the linear model does win, it is usually because the signal really is weak and linear — which, in asset pricing, is more often than you would like" (L8 p.62).

We find no detectable predictability from the characteristics, no contribution from macro, and a portfolio advantage that is not significant and does not come from the characteristics. Section 2 describes the data and the traps in it, Section 3 the design, Section 4 the results, and Section 5 what they mean.

## 2. Data

**The panel** has 50 assets in four anonymised classes and 300 month-ends (2000-01 to 2024-12):

| class | assets | median annual volatility | median Sharpe | within-class correlation | our hypothesis |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 26 | 30% | 0.25 | 0.21 | commodity-like: no country; a 54.7% loss in 2020-03 |
| B | 8 | 10% | −0.01 | 0.55 | currencies: country 7, which has no B asset, is the base |
| C | 9 | 18% | 0.24 | 0.73 | equity indices |
| D | 7 | 7% | 0.47 | 0.58 | government bonds, negatively correlated with A and C |

(Tables 3.1–3.2, Figures 3.2–3.4.) Three of the four classes are tight blocks; class A is not (within-class correlation 0.21, against 0.24 between A and B). Monthly volatility runs from 0.82% to 17.40%, which is why the target is volatility-scaled and the characteristics are ranked within class. Year-to-year Sharpe ratios of the classes are essentially unpersistent (Table 3.3, correlations −0.10 to 0.14; Figure 3.4).

**Characteristics.** Two of the five are known exactly: `x2` is the compounded return over months t−12..t−1 (99.63% of asset-months), and `x5` is the 36-month volatility (99.67%). The other three we identify from their relation to past returns and macro only (Table 3.5). For the currencies, `x1` behaves like carry (correlation 0.93 with the short-rate differential against country 7) and `x4` like the 12-month change in that differential (correlation 1.00); in the equity class `x4` correlates −0.60 with the past 12-month return instead, so it may measure something else there. `x3` behaves like value or five-year reversal: it correlates −0.93 to −0.98 with the 60-month past return in classes A, B and D. So `x2` and `x5` are exactly what the momentum and risk-parity rules use, and for the currencies `x1` and `x4` already contain country interest-rate information.

**Data problems and how they were treated** (Section 3.1):
1. **Back-fills.** Eight (variable, asset) pairs in seven assets carry copies of their first genuine value, the last dated 2002-07. They are set to missing, and the first target month is 2003-01, so none enters a model.
2. **`x10` has look-ahead.** It is constant within each calendar year and correlates 0.998 with the *same-year* average of the monthly short rate `x86`, against 0.839 with the prior-year average (Figure 3.1a). A one-month lag would leave up to 11 months of look-ahead, so we use `x86` at the common two-month macro lag.
3. **`x11` stops early** for countries 12, 9 and 11 (2020-10, 2021-08, 2024-03); a stale forward fill would be wrong by up to 10.1 percentage points (Figure 3.1b). It is nearly redundant with `x86 − x12` (pooled correlation 0.988; weakest for country 12 at 0.871), whose two parts are both in the model, so it is left out.
4. **The extended file** contains six exact copies of curated series, 26 series identical across all countries (four of them copies of the global file), 35 annual and 26 quarterly series whose values change only in January or at quarter starts, and 7 series with gaps. Only `x10` and its copy `x73` were verified to be same-period averages; the timing of the other low-frequency series is unknown, so none is used. The file was audited but never screened against returns, and only `x86` is taken from it.
5. **Two small flags, kept as stored.** `x1` is stale for five currencies in late 2024 (10 target rows); removing them moves the ridge R²_OOS only from 0.042% to 0.026% (Table 3.23). `x5` is floored at 0.004 for asset_16, but our volatility is computed from returns, so no weight uses the floor.

**Macro inputs** (Table 3.5b, a post-hoc description). The four global series look like an implied-volatility index (`x6`, peaking in 2008-11 and correlated 0.60 and 0.62 with the trailing realised volatility of classes A and C), a smoothed stress index (`x7`), a standardised activity indicator (`x8`, mean 0.23, sd 1.01) and a pro-cyclical level such as a yield (`x9`). The country series look like the short rate (`x86`), CPI inflation (`x12`, peaking in 2022–23 in all 12 countries), the change in the effective exchange rate (`x13`), policy uncertainty (`x14`), geopolitical risk (`x15`) and a country risk rating (`x16`). These are our readings from the series themselves. Every series used updates within the year (at most 9% of its changes fall in January), unlike the January-stamped `x10`.

**Features** (Table 3.7). The five characteristics are lagged (`x2` and `x5` are stored already lagged) and ranked within class and month. Global macro (log `x6`, `x7`, `x8`, `x9`) enters as trailing 60-month z-scores, lagged two months, with a separate slope for each class. Country macro (`x86`, `x12`, `x13`, log `x14`, log `x15`, `x16`) enters as the differential against country 7, turned into a trailing z-score per country so that static country gaps cannot pose as macro, lagged two months, with a slope per class for B, C and D. Class A has no natural country and gets no country macro, and assets in country 7 have a zero differential by construction. The characteristics-only set (M2) has 8 columns including 3 class intercepts; the full set (M3) has 42.

**A first look, training block only** (Tables 3.6 and 3.6b). The strongest within-class tercile spreads are value in the bond class (t = 3.22, just above the Bonferroni value of 3.18 for 25 tests), low volatility in class A (t = −2.71) and momentum in the bond class (t = −2.10). No design choice was made from these tables.

## 3. Methodology

**Design and timing.** The windows, target, benchmarks, models, decision rules and the list of 38 specifications were written down before any model was fitted (Section 3.0). They were written in the development scripts first; version control shows them only in a commit made after the development ledger had run (Section 3.10). The first target month is 2003-01, because the 36-month volatility that scales the target first exists then and the last back-fill is dated 2002-07. The initial training block, 2003-01 to 2010-12, is 96 months and includes the 2008 crisis. The out-of-sample window, 2011-01 to 2024-12, is 168 months and 8,400 asset-months, split into two seven-year halves. The pre-registered ledger was scored once on it; the diagnostics added after review re-use it and are labelled post hoc.

**Target and benchmarks.** The target is $y_{i,t}=r_{i,t}/\hat\sigma_{i,t-1}$, with $\hat\sigma$ the 36-month standard deviation (ddof = 1) of the asset's own past excess returns. Pooled least squares weights every squared error equally, so without scaling the most volatile assets would dominate the fit (our reasoning; constant variance is the assumption of L3 p.2–3). The scaling works across classes: the monthly sd of raw returns runs from 1.9% (bonds) to 8.9% (class A), while the sd of $y$ is 1.05–1.09 in every class (Table 3.6c). It does not remove variation over time: the sd of $y$ runs from 0.76 (2017) to 1.74 (2008) (Table 3.6d). Forecasts are scored with $R^2_{OOS}=1-\sum(y-\hat y)^2/\sum(y-\bar y^{bench})^2$ (L5 p.55–57). The primary benchmark is the pooled trailing mean of $y$ through month $t-1$, updated monthly; zero, the per-class and the per-asset trailing means are always shown. The benchmark matters. Before any model was fitted (Table 3.8), the mean of $y$ fell from 0.137 in training to 0.036 out of sample, so zero beats the pooled mean by 0.32%: against zero, the pre-registered benchmark is the easier bar. The per-asset trailing mean is the easiest bar of all, because it is noisy: it loses 0.27% to the pooled mean.

**Schemes and tuning.** A static fit at 2010-12 (the exam's suggested workflow), an expanding window refit every December from 2010 to 2023 (primary; 14 refits), and a rolling 96-month window (L5 p.48–52). Hyper-parameters are chosen inside each training window, never on test data. For a window ending in December of year $b$, the model is fitted up to year $b-j$ and validated on year $b-j+1$ for $j = 3, 2, 1$; the value with the lowest mean validation MSE wins, a tie goes to the heavier penalty, and the model is refit on the whole window. The only splitter is this forward-moving date mask, and its order is asserted at every fold and refit (AI guide §4a).

**Models.**
- OLS, and **ridge** (primary; L5 p.12–16, p.60). The ridge penalty is set as α/n on the summed squared error, so it means the same at every sample size, on 36 log-spaced values from $10^{3}$ to $10^{-4}$.
- Lasso (L5 p.17–24), with sklearn's α on 33 values from $10^{-0.5}$ to $10^{-4.5}$.
- PCR: principal components of the standardised training inputs (correlation-matrix PCA; L1 p.45–74 demeans only, so standardising is our choice), then OLS on the first K ∈ {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10}; combining the two taught tools is our construction.
- A random forest with 300 trees, a third of the inputs per split and a minimum leaf of 200 observations, untuned (L8 p.41–45). The large leaf is our choice for a weak signal and departs from L8 p.44, where a forest's trees are grown deep; a deep version with a leaf of 5 tests it. The forest's inputs are the five ranks and three class dummies (M2), plus the ten macro series without class interactions (M3); a tree can form interactions itself.
- Per-class ridge, fitted separately for each class on the columns that can be non-zero for it (in M3: 9 for class A, 15 for B, C and D), and variants with within-class z-scores instead of ranks, own-history levels of each characteristic, a one-month macro lag, and `x10` at a 14-month lag.

Linear models leave the class intercepts unpenalised: $y$ and $X$ are demeaned within class on the fit rows, which equals unpenalised class dummies (unit-tested). The ridge objective penalises the slopes it contains (L5 p.12); keeping the intercepts out of it is our choice. Every scaler and PCA is fitted on training rows only (AI guide §4b), and rebuilding the features from data truncated at 2010-12 changes nothing up to that date (Section 3.2).

**Inference.** The 168 out-of-sample months are resampled with replacement 10,000 times, each month keeping its whole cross-section, and the same draws are used for every model, so comparisons are paired. The lectures bootstrap single observations and refit on each resample (L2 p.80); here the forecasts are held fixed and whole months are resampled, which is our construction. Months are treated as independent. A post-hoc check finds that the monthly losses are autocorrelated (lag 1: 0.28 for the benchmark), and a moving-block bootstrap (blocks of 12 months, our construction) gives standard errors a median 1.19 times larger (Tables 3.12c–d), so the SEs below are somewhat optimistic. Results are reported as estimate ± 2 SE (L2 p.85). With 38 candidates, a "best of the ledger" claim is judged against a Bonferroni value of 3.27 (L4 p.25–28), which is conservative because the specifications are highly dependent.

**Power.** Written with the design (Section 3.0): a within-class ranking signal with a true R² of 0.3% would give t ≈ 3.34, but a class-level (macro) signal of the same size only t ≈ 1.01, because the four classes behave like about 8 independent assets. The Q1 statistic turned out noisier than that calculation assumed, because it also carries the errors of the class intercepts. Its SE is 0.241%, so a true R²_OOS of 0.48% was needed to pass the rule half the time, and 0.68% to pass it with 80% probability (Section 3.5).

**Placebo.** Every raw macro series is circularly shifted by 36, 48, …, 120 months, and the identical pipeline runs (the exam's suggested workflow). The shift wraps around, so the first months of the sample receive values from its end; with the 60-month z-score and the two-month lag, this reaches the first 49 out-of-sample months at a 120-month shift (Table 3.19b). The wrapped values are later macro at the wrong date, which is what a placebo is meant to be, but they are not strictly past data.

**Portfolios** (unit gross exposure, monthly rebalancing). P1 holds $w_{i,t}\propto\hat y_{i,t}/\hat\sigma_{i,t-1}$; a constant forecast turns it into risk parity exactly. P3 holds $w_{i,t}\propto(\hat y_{i,t}-\bar{\hat y}_{\text{class},t})/\hat\sigma_{i,t-1}$, a long–short portfolio within each class. The benchmarks are EW, RP ∝ 1/σ̂ and TSMOM ∝ sign(R12)/σ̂. Turnover is $\sum_i|w_{i,t}-w_{i,t-1}(1+r_{i,t-1})/(1+r_{p,t-1})|$, buys plus sells against the drifted weights. Costs are 10 bp per unit of turnover, charged to every strategy (our assumption; costs are not taught), with a 0–50 bp grid and break-even costs; under the convention that one-way turnover is half the sum, this is 20 bp, so it is conservative. The class risk budget sets each class sleeve to unit gross and scales it by the inverse trailing volatility of that class's RP sleeve. Exposure versus timing is measured by regressing net P1 returns on the three net benchmark returns (α as a performance benchmark, L2 p.49; tests of α, L2 p.94–97).

**Pre-registered decision rules.** Q1: R²_OOS more than 2 SE above zero. Q2: a macro gain more than 2 SE above zero **and** above every placebo gain. Q3: a higher net Sharpe ratio than all three rules **and** α > 0 with t > 2.

## 4. Results

### 4.1 Forecastability (Q1)

**Table A. Out-of-sample R², 2011–2024 (from Table 3.10b)**

| specification | vs pooled trailing mean (SE) | vs zero | vs per-asset trailing mean |
|---|---|---|---|
| class means only (OLS M1, expanding) | 0.09% (0.24%) | −0.23% | 0.36% |
| **ridge on characteristic ranks (M2, expanding; pre-registered)** | **0.04% (0.24%)** | **−0.28%** | **0.32%** |
| ridge with macro (M3, expanding) | −0.08% (0.25%) | −0.40% | 0.19% |
| random forest (M2, expanding) | −0.04% (0.17%) | −0.36% | 0.23% |
| random forest (M2, rolling; best of 38) | 0.21% (0.36%) | −0.11% | 0.48% |
| OLS with macro (M3, static) | −20.44% (3.32%) | −20.83% | −20.11% |
| deep forest with macro (M3, expanding) | −6.16% (3.53%) | −6.50% | −5.87% |

**The headline is no.** The pre-registered model scores 0.042% (SE 0.241%) against the pooled trailing mean and −0.279% (SE 0.570%) against zero, and the rule fails. On its own this says little, because the statistic could only have detected an R²_OOS of about 0.5%.

**What the characteristics add to class means.** The class-means model alone scores 0.087% (SE 0.236%), and on the same months the characteristics change that by −0.045% (SE 0.027%). The ±2 SE interval runs from −0.099% to +0.009%, so this is a non-rejection that rules out a gain of more than about 0.01% *from the characteristic part ridge actually fitted*. That part was heavily shrunk: in 64% of the refits ridge chose the heaviest penalty, which leaves only the class means (93% once macro is added; Table 3.14). The unshrunk OLS version does worse than the class means (−0.01% against 0.09%, Table 3.10b). So the paired test shows that the characteristics, as the models used them, did not help. It is not a bound on every within-class signal a different model might find.

**The ledger as a whole.** The best specification, a forest with rolling windows, scores 0.21% (SE 0.36%), far from the Bonferroni bar, and every positive value is inside one SE. None of the 38 beats a zero forecast. These are highly dependent estimates that share one fact, the fall in the mean of $y$ after 2010. Against each asset's own trailing mean the picture looks better (Table 3.10c): class means score +0.36% (SE 0.165%, t = 2.18), ridge M2 +0.32% (t = 1.92), and 17 of 38 specifications are positive; with the block bootstrap the class-means t is 3.06 (Table 3.12d), still below the Bonferroni bar. That is the gain from shrinking 50 noisy asset means toward four class means, not predictability from characteristics.

**Flexible models lose.** Kitchen-sink OLS on the 42 columns fits its training windows (in-sample R² 7.67% static, 4.66% expanding) but scores −20.44% and −4.71% out of sample (L4 p.47–48). The deep forests are worse than the leaf-200 forest on the same months: −2.19% (SE 0.36%) with characteristics only and −4.69% (SE 2.41%) with macro (Table 3.21). With a signal this weak, averaging deep trees does not remove enough variance (consistent with L8 p.62). The leaf-200 forest scores −0.78% to +0.21% across schemes with characteristics only, and −3.74% to −0.74% with macro. In all, 29 of the 38 specifications lie between −0.97% and +0.21%; the other nine are the three macro OLS fits, the static and expanding macro forests, the two deep forests, per-class ridge with macro (−1.17%) and PCR with five fixed components (−1.13%). Lasso keeps no variable in half of its refits. PCR mostly picks one component, the edge of its pre-registered grid; allowed K = 0 (class means only, a post-hoc check), it picks 0 in 8 of 14 refits and scores −0.095% instead of −0.171% (Section 3.6). Its components are almost pure macro: the characteristics carry at most 0.003 of the squared loadings of the first three components in any window (Table 3.14d), so PCR's result is evidence about macro (Q2), not about the characteristics.

**Tuning is not the reason** (post hoc; Table 3.14c, Figures 3.5c–d). Scoring every grid value on the test months, as if it had been fixed in advance, gives an upper bound chosen with hindsight. The best fixed value scores 0.087% for ridge M2 (a heavy penalty, α/n ≈ 25), 0.086% for ridge M3 and 0.087% for lasso (both at the class-means end of the grid itself) and 0.088% for PCR (one component). Class means alone score 0.087%. So no value on any grid beats the class means by more than about 0.001 percentage points.

**No exploitable nonlinearity** (post hoc; Table 3.12e). On the same features, scheme and months, the forest minus ridge is −0.083% (SE 0.130%) on characteristics with expanding windows, and within about one SE in five of the six pairs; the exception is the static macro forest, which is worse (t = −2.08).

**The forecasts carry almost no information.** Their standard deviation is 0.055 against 1.043 for $y$, their correlation with outcomes is 0.020, and the forecast deciles show no clear relation to outcomes: their mean realised $y$ is not monotone in the forecast (Figure 3.6b, Table 3.17b). The cumulative gain of the class-means and ridge models over the trailing mean builds up until 2020 and is mostly given back in 2021–22, while a zero forecast gains more than any model (Figure 3.6a).

**Schemes, classes and characteristics.** The static fit is worst for every model: on the same months, static minus expanding is −0.727% for ridge M2 (paired SE 0.385%, t = −1.89), with t = −2.47 for OLS, −2.10 for the forest and −1.89 for PCR (Table 3.12b). Rolling and expanding windows differ by less than one SE in every model. By class, the class intercepts help currencies (+1.8% for ridge M2, 1.9% for class means alone) and hurt bonds (−1.4%; Table 3.11). In raw-return units the largest positive numbers are for the equity-like class, whose positive mean beats a zero forecast by about 2.5% (Table 3.13): that is the equity premium, not a characteristic. Three ridge coefficients keep their sign in every refit: low volatility (`u5`, negative), carry (`u1`) and the change in carry (`u4`) (Table 3.15). This is weak evidence, because the expanding windows share most of their rows and at the heaviest penalty a coefficient's sign is essentially that of its univariate covariance with $y$. For the characteristics-only forest, the class dummies and volatility carry the largest out-of-sample importance; with macro, the global series `x7` ranks first (Table 3.16). Separate models per class are no better than the pooled model with characteristics only (+0.001%, SE 0.034%) and much worse with macro (−1.086%, SE 0.386%; Table 3.17), because each class has only 672–864 training rows at the first refit for 15 slopes.

### 4.2 Does macro add? (Q2)

**Table B. Macro against placebos (ridge, expanding; Tables 3.18, 3.19, 3.21)**

| comparison | gain in R²_OOS (SE) |
|---|---|
| **with macro minus without (pre-registered)** | **−0.123% (0.085%)** |
| global macro only | −0.414% |
| country macro only | −0.106% |
| eight placebos (macro shifted 36–120 months) | −0.563% to +0.125% |
| real macro's rank among real + 8 placebos | 4 of 9 |
| curated `x10` at a 14-month lag, minus `x86` | +0.146% (0.069%) |
| characteristic × macro interactions minus ridge M2 (post hoc) | +0.012% (0.089%); rank 6 of 9 against its own placebos |

**No.** Adding macro lowers R²_OOS in all 14 paired comparisons of Table 3.18, slightly for ridge and heavily for OLS (−19.47% static) and the forests (up to −3.93% for the deep forest). The real macro series rank 4th of 9 against the placebos, so correctly timed macro does no better than macro shifted by three to ten years, and the rule fails on both of its conditions. The one positive macro comparison is the curated `x10` variant against the `x86` version: +0.146% (SE 0.069%), one of nine robustness checks; with the block bootstrap its t falls from 2.09 to 1.24 (Table 3.12d), and that model still scores only 0.065%. Among macro models without a no-macro twin, lasso on rolling windows scores 0.18% (SE 0.88%), well inside noise. A one-month macro lag is worse (−0.372%), and own-history characteristic levels do not help. Interactions of the five characteristic ranks with the four global macro series, the one macro channel that varies within a class, were checked after the audit as a labelled 39th specification (Table 3.19c): they add +0.012% (SE 0.089%) to ridge M2, rank 6th of 9 against their own placebos, and ridge sits at its no-signal end in 6 of 14 refits. The `x10` leak demonstration, with the look-ahead left in, scores −0.050% (Table 3.22): the leak would not have produced a false success here, but it would still have been an invalid design.

Two features of the design favour macro. Country macro varies across countries within a month (20–80% of its variance in classes B–D is cross-country; Table 3.18b), so it is not only a class-level signal. And the class-B characteristics `x1` and `x4` are same-month functions of short rates used at a one-month lag, as the exam's rule for characteristics allows, while macro gets two months for release delays; that gives the characteristics a small timing edge, and they still add nothing.

### 4.3 Portfolios (Q3)

**Table C. Portfolios, 2011–2024, net of 10 bp (Tables 3.24–3.25)**

| portfolio | net Sharpe | 2011–17 | 2018–24 | turnover / month |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **P1 on ridge M2 (pre-registered)** | **0.44** | 0.58 | 0.32 | 4.2% |
| P1 on the class-means model | 0.44 | 0.58 | 0.32 | 3.8% |
| P1 on ridge M2, static (2010 forecasts frozen; diagnostic) | 0.27 | 0.33 | 0.21 | 3.6% |
| P1 on ridge M2, rolling (not selected; diagnostic) | 0.60 | 0.66 | 0.53 | 8.2% |
| RP | 0.30 | 0.37 | 0.24 | 3.6% |
| EW | 0.28 | −0.02 | 0.53 | 4.1% |
| TSMOM | 0.12 | 0.29 | −0.06 | 26.5% |
| P3, within-class long–short | −0.11 | −0.07 | −0.17 | 34.5% |

**P1 beats all three rules on net Sharpe ratio, but it does not pass the pre-registered test.** Regressing net P1 on the three net benchmark returns gives α = 0.61% a year with t = 1.74 (Table 3.27), and the Sharpe gain over RP is 0.14 with a bootstrap SE of 0.11 (Table 3.29). The rules explain 90% of P1's variance jointly. The individual β's are not separate exposures: EW and RP returns correlate 0.93 (Table 3.27d), and RP's β is 1.21 next to EW but 0.72 on its own (L3 p.39–44). P1's advantage survives costs up to about 260 bp against EW and 363 bp against RP (Table 3.26). P3, the only portfolio that bets on the characteristics within a class, earns almost nothing before costs (gross Sharpe ratio 0.08), and its 34% monthly turnover makes it lose after them. The rolling version is not a result: it was not the pre-registered scheme and is shown only to indicate how much the portfolio depends on the scheme.

**How fragile is the α?** (post hoc; Tables 3.27c–e, Figure 3.9). Six months have a studentized residual beyond ±2.5 (L3 p.16); the largest, 2011-09, also has the highest leverage. Without them the α would be 0.69% a year with t = 2.20. That is reported, not used: L3 p.17 allows deleting a point only for a good reason, and the verdict follows the pre-registered rule on all 168 months. Resampling the months and refitting (L2 p.80) gives almost the same SE for α as the textbook formula (t = 1.74 either way). So the α is not significant, and whether it looks significant depends on six months.

**Where P1's return comes from.** Not from the characteristics: P1 on the class-means model holds almost the same weights (average monthly weight correlation 0.9994), and with it as a fourth regressor its β is 0.95, the R² is 0.998 and α disappears (t = 0.82). Nor from a fixed tilt: the class means are re-estimated every December and the tilt moves with them (Table 3.28b). The forecast of $y$ for the bond class rises from 0.169 in 2011 to 0.216 in 2015 and falls to 0.125 in 2024, while the currency forecast falls from 0.122 to −0.011; class D's share of P1's gross exposure goes from 45% (2011) to 74% (2021) and back to 46% (2024). Frozen at its 2010 estimates, the same model earns a net Sharpe ratio of 0.27 and a negative α (−0.16% a year, t = −0.91). The point estimates therefore associate P1's advantage with slowly updated class premia, which the three rules do not use. The differences are not precise: P1 minus its frozen version is 0.18 with a bootstrap SE of 0.11 (Table 3.29).

**The tilt is concentrated and its timing is uneven.** On average P1 puts 60% of its gross exposure in the bond class, against 37% for RP, and asset_16, the lowest-volatility bond, averages 22% of gross exposure and reaches 42%. Against the three rules, α is −0.16% a year (t = −0.37) in 2011–17 and +1.36% (t = 2.47) in 2018–24 (Table 3.27b; the halves were fixed in the design, the regression by half was added after review). P1's own Sharpe ratio fell from 0.58 to 0.32 between the halves, but the rules moved too, and in 2018–24 EW (0.53) beats P1. The correlation between the equity and bond classes turned from −0.23 (2011–19) to +0.22 (2020–24; Table 3.30); Table 3.30's periods do not match the halves, so this is only suggestive for P1's lower Sharpe ratio. Budgeting risk equally across classes cuts class D to 46% and the net Sharpe ratio to 0.41.

## 5. Discussion

**Why there is so little.** Monthly returns are mostly news (L1 p.33), and with 168 months and 50 assets in correlated blocks the headline test could only detect an R²_OOS of about 0.5%, above the range the literature reports. The characteristics were given a fair chance beyond the rules: `x2` and `x5` enter the benchmarks as time-series signals, asset by asset, while a within-class bet on their ranks is a different, cross-sectional bet. P3 is the within-class bet on the fitted characteristic forecast, dominated by volatility (Table 3.15), and it earns a gross Sharpe ratio of 0.08. Global macro is one number per month and enters at the class level, where the power calculation gave only about 8 independent assets. Country macro does vary across countries, but it is partly duplicated inside `x1` and `x4` for the currencies. With 34 extra slopes, the macro block raises in-sample fit and lowers out-of-sample fit (Table 3.10b), the pattern of estimation error, and the placebos do as well as the real series.

**What the models did learn** is that average risk-adjusted returns differ across classes and drift. The bond class had the highest class mean in the 2003–2010 data (0.169, Table 3.28b), and the currency class's premium kept shrinking afterwards. A portfolio that follows the running class means beat risk parity on the point estimate; the same tilt frozen in 2010 did not.

**Clear and fragile evidence.** The clear evidence is negative: the characteristics, as fitted, add nothing to class means; macro loses in every paired comparison and matches its placebos; the flexible learners do worse on the same months than their shrunk counterparts (Tables 3.10b, 3.21). This is "not detectable" rather than "shown to be zero". The positive evidence is fragile. Every positive R²_OOS against the pooled mean is inside one SE. A few results pass 2 SE (class means against the per-asset mean, t = 2.18; the curated `x10` variant, 0.146% with SE 0.069%; the 2018–24 α, t = 2.47), but none comes near the Bonferroni bar and each is secondary or post hoc; with a 12-month block bootstrap the `x10` variant falls to t = 1.24. The portfolio's Sharpe advantage over RP is 1.3 SEs, depends on re-estimating the class means, appears in α only after 2018, leans on one asset, and its α's apparent significance hinges on six months.

**Considered and not used, and why.**
- *Boosted trees* (L8 p.48–55): rounds, depth and learning rate would have to be tuned on the same forward folds, which is possible without leakage but multiplies the runtime; L8 p.55 recommends the forest when time is short. The forest already shows no sign of exploitable nonlinearity (forest minus ridge within about one SE in five of six pairs, Table 3.12e), so boosting was left out for runtime.
- *KNN and neural networks*: the course does not teach KNN regression (Lecture 6 teaches the KNN classifier), and neural networks are only named, so both are outside the scope rule.
- *A classification framing* (logistic regression, KNN, Lecture 6): Q1 asks how much of the return is forecastable, a continuous question; a sign classifier would answer a different one.
- *Clustering* (Lecture 7): the class labels are given. Clustering the assets would be exploratory, and would have to use training data only.
- *Covariance-optimised weights*: not taught, and a 50 × 50 sample covariance is rank-deficient on a 36-month window (L1 p.63); the class risk budget uses only sleeve volatilities.
- *Information criteria* (AIC, BIC; L4 p.29–32): they assume independent rows, and rows in the same month are strongly correlated; forward validation folds tune on the question actually asked.
- *Screening the extended file* against returns (L4 p.25–28) and *outside data*: no hypothesis to test, a larger search, and for the anonymised assets and countries no way to align outside series.

**Not tried.** Cross-country macro transforms, such as ranking the 12 countries each month, were not in the pre-registered list and were not run. Characteristic × macro interactions, which were also outside the list, were run after the audit (Section 4.2) and add nothing.

**Limitations.** The data are anonymised and the universe of 50 assets is fixed (survivorship). The macro series are revised data, not real-time vintages. The cost is one uniform number, although bonds and currencies are cheaper to trade than commodities. The month bootstrap holds the forecasts fixed and treats months as independent; the block-bootstrap check suggests its SEs are about a fifth too small (median ratio 1.19). The exam's setup cell silences warnings, which we kept, so a future library deprecation would not be shown; the forests dominate the runtime.

**Next steps.** Class-specific transaction costs; point-in-time macro vintages; a longer history or a wider universe, so that a class-level signal of 0.3% becomes detectable; a weight cap for the risk budget; and a fresh holdout period to test the one hypothesis this study generates: that tilting risk parity toward slowly updated class premia earns more than risk parity.

## 6. Conclusion

**Is next month's return forecastable from the five characteristics? Not detectably.** Ridge on the characteristics explains 0.04% of the volatility-scaled return against the trailing mean (SE 0.24%). On top of class means, the characteristics add −0.045% (SE 0.027%), and no model we tried beats a forecast of zero.

**Does macro help? No.** Adding global and country macro lowers out-of-sample R² in every paired comparison, macro shifted to the wrong years does just as well, and letting macro modulate the characteristics adds nothing.

**Should a desk pay for the forecast portfolio?** It earned a net Sharpe ratio of 0.44 in 2011–2024, against 0.30 for risk parity, 0.28 for equal weight and 0.12 for time-series momentum. But the characteristics contribute nothing to it: it is risk parity plus a class tilt that follows the running class means, on average 60% of exposure in the bond class and 22% in a single bond. Frozen at its 2010 estimate, the tilt does worse than risk parity. Its α over the three rules is 0.61% a year with t = 1.74, negative in 2011–17 and positive only in 2018–24, and its apparent significance depends on six months. A desk can build the same portfolio from running class average returns and risk parity, without a characteristic model. If it wants that bet, it should size it as a deliberate, slowly updated class tilt with its own concentration limits, and test it on data it has not yet seen.
